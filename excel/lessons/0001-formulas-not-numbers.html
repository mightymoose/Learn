Lesson 01 · Tier 1, Foundations

Formulas, not numbers

The one habit that separates a spreadsheet from a calculator. Type the address of a number, never the number itself.

After this lesson

You can write a formula that refers to other cells, explain why the cell and the formula bar show different things, and change one input and watch every answer update by itself.

The grid is a set of addresses

A worksheet is a grid. Columns have letters across the top, rows have numbers down the side. Every cell has an address made of the two: column letter first, then row number. The cell where column C meets row 2 is called C2.

That address is the whole idea. It gives you a way to say the number that lives there without saying what the number is.

Here is the data this whole tier uses. Six sales reps, three regions. Open a blank workbook and type it in now. Typing it yourself takes two minutes and it is worth it, because every later lesson refers back to it.

An equals sign changes everything

Type 540 into a cell and Excel stores the number 540. Type =120*4.5 and Excel stores an instruction, works it out, and shows you 540.

The leading = is what makes the difference. It tells Excel the rest is a formula, not text. Without it you get the literal characters back.

Now the important version. Click cell E2 and type this.

=C2*D2

Press Enter. You get 540 again. It looks like the same answer, and it is not the same thing at all.

The cell shows the answer, the formula bar shows the work

Click on E2 once. The cell still reads 540. Look at the long white strip above the column letters, the formula bar. It reads =C2*D2.

This trips up every beginner exactly once. The grid shows you results. The formula bar shows you what produced them. When you open somebody else's workbook and a number looks wrong, this is where you look first.

Cell E2 contains =C2*D2. What does the cell itself show, and what does the formula bar show?

Why the reference matters more than the answer

Both =120*4.5 and =C2*D2 give 540 today. Now change Ana's units. Click C2 and type 150.

The formula holding C2*D2 becomes 675 on its own. The formula holding 120*4.5 still says 540, and it is now wrong. It is wrong silently, with no error and no colour, which is the worst way for a number to be wrong.

A typed number is a copy. A reference is a live link. Excel keeps track of which cells feed which formulas and recalculates the ones that need it, every time anything changes.

The rule

If a number could ever change, it belongs in its own cell, and every formula points at that cell. Numbers buried inside formulas are the single most common reason a spreadsheet quietly goes wrong.

You typed =120*4.5 into a cell. Ana's units then change from 120 to 150. What happens to your cell?

Excel does not read left to right

One more thing before you practise. Excel applies operators in a fixed order, not in reading order. Brackets first, then exponents, then multiply and divide, then add and subtract.

So =2+3*4 is 14, not 20. If you meant 20, you have to say so.

=(2+3)*4

This bites hardest with averages and percentages. When a formula mixes + with * or /, add the brackets even when they are not strictly needed. They cost nothing and they say what you meant.

Do this now

In the workbook you just typed, build a Revenue column.

Click E1 and type Revenue.

Click E2. Type =, then click cell C2, type *, then click cell D2, then press Enter. Clicking builds the reference for you and is faster than typing it.

Do the same for rows 3 to 7. Yes, this is tedious. Lesson 2 is entirely about never doing it this way again.

Change C2 to 150. Watch E2 change by itself. That moment is the whole lesson.
