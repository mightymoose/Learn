Lesson 03 · Tier 1, Foundations

Tables

One keystroke turns a block of cells into an object that knows how big it is. After this, adding 500 rows stops breaking everything.

After this lesson

You can convert a range into a Table, write a formula once and have it apply to the whole column, read a formula like =SUM(Sales[Revenue]), and add new data at the bottom without touching a single formula.

The problem left over from lesson 2

Your commission column works. Now somebody sends you next month's file with 500 more rows, and you paste them in below row 7.

Nothing calculates. The formulas stop at row 7, because you filled them to row 7. Your total, if you wrote =SUM(E2:E7), still adds six rows out of 506 and reports a confident, wrong number.

You could fill down again every time. People do this for years. The fix takes one keystroke.

Ctrl and T

Click any cell inside your data. Press Ctrl T. On a Mac it is the same keys. A small box appears asking whether your table has headers. It does, so leave that ticked, and press Enter.

The block gains banded colours and little filter arrows in the header row. That cosmetic change is not the point. The point is that Excel now treats those cells as one named object rather than as a coincidence of adjacent cells.

Rename it immediately. With a cell inside the Table selected, go to the Table Design tab and type Sales in the Table Name box. The default name is Table1, which tells nobody anything.

One name for one thing

In this course a Table with a capital T always means the object made by Ctrl T. A plain block of cells is a range. The whole lesson is the difference between the two, so the words never get swapped. See the glossary.

Three things a Table does for you

1. It grows

Type anything in the row directly below the last one. The Table swallows it. The banding extends, and every formula column fills itself in for the new row. Paste 500 rows and the same thing happens 500 times.

2. Its columns have names

Click an empty cell outside the Table and start typing =SUM(. Now select the whole Revenue column by clicking its header. Excel does not write E2:E7. It writes this.

=SUM(Sales[Revenue])

That is a structured reference. Read it as the Revenue column of the Table called Sales. It carries no row numbers at all, which is exactly why 500 new rows cannot break it. Microsoft documents the form in Overview of Excel tables.

Inside the Table, a formula refers to the current row with @. Delete your Revenue formula and type this into any Revenue cell instead.

=[@Units]*[@Price]

Press Enter and Excel fills the entire column at once. There is no filling down any more. The column is one formula, and Excel calls it a calculated column.

3. It can total itself

On the Table Design tab, tick Total Row. A row appears at the bottom. Click any cell in it and a small dropdown offers Sum, Average, Count and more. The total row moves down on its own as the Table grows.

Your data is now a Table with a calculated Revenue column. A colleague pastes 500 new rows at the bottom. What happens to the Revenue formula?

What does =SUM(Sales[Revenue]) buy you over =SUM(E2:E7)?

What Tables cost you

Two honest warnings, because a lesson that only sells a feature is not teaching.

A Table cannot have merged cells, and it wants one heading per column with no blanks. If your data has a title row above the headings or two rows of headings, clean that up first. This is a feature, not a limitation. Data laid out that way was already going to cause trouble.

Structured references do not fill sideways the way ordinary ones do. If you drag =[@Units] across, it does not step to the next column. That is rarely what you want inside a Table, but it surprises people once.

Do this now

Click inside your data and press Ctrl T. Confirm the headers box is ticked.

On the Table Design tab, rename it from Table1 to Sales.

Replace the Revenue formula with =[@Units]*[@Price]. Watch the whole column fill at once.

Tick Total Row, and set the Revenue total to Sum. You should see 3427.50.

Now type a new rep in row 8, above the total row. Revenue and Commission calculate themselves, and the total updates. Nothing had to be filled down.

Put the conversion in order, from a raw pasted range to a working Table.

Ask your teacher. Good questions here: what to do when your source data has two header rows, whether to convert a Table back to a range and how, or why your calculated column shows a small warning triangle.
