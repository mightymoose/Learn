Lesson 01 · Lang, Chapter I, §11

Universal objects

The pattern a paper uses when it says “by the universal property of” and then moves on.

After this lesson

You can read a definition of the form there exists a unique morphism making the diagram commute, name the category it lives in, say whether the object attracts or repels, and get uniqueness up to a unique isomorphism without doing any work.

Why this comes first

Your mission is to read modern algebra papers. Papers do not rebuild standard objects. They name a universal property, use it, and move on. A first course teaches you to build the tensor product. A paper expects you to already own it.

Lang puts this pattern in Chapter I, §11, before rings and before modules. That placement is deliberate. He then reuses the same pattern for products, coproducts, direct and inverse limits, the tensor product in Chapter XVI, the alternating product in Chapter XIX, and Clifford algebras. One pattern, used about ten times.

What Lang actually says

Let ℂ be a category. An object P of ℂ is called universally attracting if there exists a unique morphism of each object of ℂ into P, and is called universally repelling if for every object of ℂ there exists a unique morphism of P into this object.

Lang, Algebra, 3rd rev. ed., p. 57

Read the direction carefully. It is the only thing that separates the two words.

The arrow points at P, or away from it. Everything else follows from that.

Lang then drops both adverbs. When the direction is clear from context he writes only universal, and so will these lessons.

Lang notes on p. 58 that ℤ is universally repelling in the category of rings. What does that assert?

The payoff, and why it is free

Here is the sentence that makes the definition worth learning.

Since a universal object P admits the identity morphism into itself, it is clear that if P, P′ are two universal objects in ℂ, then there exists a unique isomorphism between them.

Lang, Algebra, 3rd rev. ed., p. 58

Lang compresses the proof into one clause. Papers compress it to nothing. Write it out once, in the attracting case, and you never need to write it again.

P′ is an object and P attracts, so there is a unique u : P′ → P.
P is an object and P′ attracts, so there is a unique v : P → P′.
Now u ∘ v and the identity are both morphisms from P to P.
P attracts, so only one such morphism exists. Therefore u ∘ v = id, and the same argument gives v ∘ u = id.

So u is an isomorphism. It is unique, because it was the only morphism P′ → P to begin with. Note what carried the argument. The identity morphism did all the work.

Why papers care

Uniqueness up to a unique isomorphism is stronger than uniqueness up to isomorphism. Two cyclic groups of order 10 are isomorphic, but not uniquely, because the isomorphism depends on which generator you pick. A universal object has no such choice left in it. That is why a paper can say “the” tensor product and treat any two constructions as the same object.

The move that makes it general

Most universal properties in the wild do not look like Lang's definition. They look like the product. Lang defines it on p. 58.

By a product of A, B … one means a triple (P, f, g) … Given two morphisms φ : C → A and ψ : C → B … there exists a unique morphism h : C → P which makes the following diagram commutative.

Lang, Algebra, 3rd rev. ed., p. 58

The dashed arrow is the one the property hands you. φ = f ∘ h and ψ = g ∘ h.

That statement has no universal object in it. There is no category in which P attracts everything, because P is just an object of an ordinary category. Lang fixes this on the next page. He builds a category to fit.

We note that the product of A, B is universal in the category whose objects consist of pairs of morphisms f : C → A and g : C → B …

Lang, Algebra, 3rd rev. ed., p. 59

That is the move. An object of the new category is a whole cone (C, f, g), not just C. A morphism from (C, f, g) to (C′, f′, g′) is a map h : C → C′ that respects both legs. In that category the product is universally attracting, and the uniqueness argument you just rebuilt applies unchanged.

Lang gives the same treatment on p. 58 to free abelian groups. The free abelian group on a set S is universal in the category whose objects are maps S → A into abelian groups. Same shape, different category.

You meet a new construction stated as “there is a unique h : C → P with f ∘ h = φ and g ∘ h = ψ”. What are the objects of the category you should build?

Before the next lesson

Do not reread this page. Two days from now, answer these from memory. Getting them back with effort is what makes them stick.

  1. State both halves of Lang's definition, and get the arrow directions right.
  2. Prove that two universal objects are uniquely isomorphic, in four steps.
  3. Say which category makes the product a universal object.
  4. The coproduct reverses every arrow. Is it attracting or repelling?
