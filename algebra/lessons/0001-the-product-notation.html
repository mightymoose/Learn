Lesson 01 · Lang, Chapter I, §1, pp. 3–7

The product notation

You already know what a monoid is. This is about the machinery Lang builds on top of it, which the next 900 pages assume.

After this lesson

You can say exactly what ∏i∈I f(i) means in Lang, name the two theorems that make it well defined, and say why x0 = e is forced rather than chosen.

Why §1 is not really about monoids

Lang defines a monoid on p. 3 in four lines. A set with an associative law of composition and a unit element. You met that years ago. If §1 were about monoids it would end there.

It runs to p. 7. The rest builds one thing: a product symbol that works over an arbitrary index set. Direct sums in §7, polynomials in Chapter II, and every coordinate-wise argument in the book run on it. Lang states its rules once, here, and never restates them.

So read §1 for the notation, not for the definition.

Three moves, in order

1. The n-fold product is defined, not assumed

Lang defines the product of x1, …, xn by induction, on p. 4. Each new factor attaches on the right.

∏ν=1n xν = (x1 ··· xn−1)xn

That single expression is one particular way to bracket the factors. Associativity as stated in the definition of a monoid only moves brackets among three elements. It does not say the other bracketings of n factors agree. Lang closes the gap with a rule on p. 4.

∏μ=1m xμ · ∏ν=1n xm+ν = ∏ν=1m+n xν

He says this rule lets you insert parentheses any way you like, then leaves the proof as an exercise. It is an easy induction on n. Do it once and the word “obviously” stops appearing in your own proofs at this level.

2. The empty product is the unit element

Lang sets the product of no factors equal to e, on p. 4. This looks like bookkeeping. It is doing real work.

On p. 6 he defines powers by xn = ∏1n x, for every integer n ≥ 0. Feed in n = 0 and you get x0 = e with no separate stipulation. The convention on p. 4 already decided it.

The habit worth copying

A convention that removes a special case is usually the right convention. Lang picks the empty product so that xn+m = xnxm holds at n = 0 without a side condition. Watch for the same move with empty sums, empty direct sums, and the zero module.

Why does Lang get x0 = e without stating it as a separate rule?

3. The index set need not be ordered, or finite

This is the payoff of §1, on p. 5. Let G be a commutative monoid, let I be any set, and let f : I → G be a map with f(i) = e for almost all i. Lang fixes that phrase on the same page to mean all but a finite number.

Write I0 for the set of i with f(i) ≠ e. Then I0 is finite, and Lang defines

∏i∈I f(i) = ∏i∈I0 f(i)

Only the filled points do anything. There may be infinitely many hollow ones, and the product is still a finite product.

Two facts have to hold before that definition is legal. First, I0 is finite, so the right side is a product of finitely many factors. Second, I0 carries no order, so the value must not depend on the order you choose. Lang proves that second fact on p. 5 for commutative monoids, by induction, and this is the one proof in §1 he writes out in full rather than leaving as an exercise.

Note the hypothesis

Order independence needs commutativity. Lang states the reordering theorem for a commutative monoid, and the index-set product inherits that hypothesis. This is why ∏i∈I appears in Lang for abelian groups and modules, and why non-commutative arguments keep an explicit ordering.

Which condition makes ∏i∈If(i) legal when I is infinite?

The rest of §1, in one pass

Lang closes the section with material you can read quickly. He states a Fubini rule for double products on p. 6 and leaves it as an exercise. He defines products of subsets SS′ and notes GG = G. He defines a submonoid as a subset that contains e and is closed under the law of composition.

Two examples there are worth marking. The multiplicative subsets of a commutative ring are monoids, which is the definition localization will need in Chapter II, §4. And the connected sum of compact surfaces makes a monoid, which is Lang showing you the notion is not confined to algebra.
