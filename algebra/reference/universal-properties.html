Ahead of the sequence

The lessons run through Lang in order from Chapter I, §1. This card covers §11, so it arrives before its lesson does. The content is verified and you can use it now. Note that §11 leans on free abelian groups from §7 and on the direct and inverse limits of §10.

The two definitions

NameCondition on PArrows
Universally attracting For each object X, exactly one morphism X → P. point at P
Universally repelling For each object X, exactly one morphism P → X. leave P

Lang writes universal for either one when the direction is clear (p. 58). Elsewhere you will meet terminal for attracting and initial for repelling. They mean the same thing.

The theorem you get for free

Two universal objects in the same category are isomorphic by a unique isomorphism. (Lang, p. 58.)

Proof in the attracting case, four steps:

Unique u : P′ → P, because P attracts.
Unique v : P → P′, because P′ attracts.
u ∘ v and id both run P → P.
Only one such morphism exists, so u ∘ v = id. Symmetrically v ∘ u = id.

The identity morphism is the hinge. Reverse every arrow for the repelling case.

Why the word “unique” matters twice

Unique isomorphism is stronger than isomorphic. Two cyclic groups of order n are isomorphic, but the isomorphism depends on a choice of generator. A universal object leaves no choice, so a paper may write “the” product and identify any two constructions of it.

Recipe: turn a stated property into a universal object

Find the data the property quantifies over. For the product that is a triple (C, φ, ψ), not just C.
Make that data the objects of a new category.
Make a morphism the underlying map that respects every leg.
Read the arrow direction off the “unique h” clause. Into the object means attracting, out of it means repelling.

In the category of sets the product is the cartesian product with its projections (Lang, p. 59). In the category of groups it is the direct product with its projection homomorphisms (Lang, p. 59).
